## 0:00 – 0:15 · Hook

`[ON SCREEN: Ayodhya at dawn, slow push-in. Label top-left: SB 9.6]`

You have heard of Ayodhya. Almost nobody knows why its first king threw out his own eldest son
before he died.

`[ON SCREEN: hard cut. A hand drops a half-eaten rabbit onto a leaf plate.]`

It was over one rabbit. And the son he threw out is the reason Rama has one of his names.

## 0:15 – 0:45 · The turn

`[ON SCREEN: series card: THE SUN DYNASTY · ARC 1 · IKSHVAKU]`

This is the story of Ikshvaku, the first king of the Sun dynasty, the line Rama was born into
dozens of generations later. We are going to tell it the way the Bhagavata Purana tells it, in
the ninth book, chapter six. Whenever a detail comes from somewhere else, I will say so.

Three things happen in this episode: a birth nobody expected, a punishment that seems far too
harsh, and a name that Rama ends up carrying.

## 0:45 – 2:15 · Beat 1: born from a sneeze

`[ON SCREEN: illustrated Manu seated in meditation, the sun disc behind him. Small family-tree strip along the bottom: Vivasvan → Manu → ?]`

Start with the father. His name is Vaivasvata Manu, the son of Vivasvan, the sun god. That is
where "Sun dynasty" comes from. Every king in this series descends from the sun through him. If
you watched episode one, you already know Manu: he is the king a small fish once asked for
protection, and that fish carried him through the flood.

`[ON SCREEN: the fish from E01, brief callback, two seconds]`

Manu wants sons. In the Bhagavata's account, the first of them is born in a way that has no
parallel anywhere else in the book.

`[ON SCREEN: close on Manu's face. Light bursts outward from it.]`

Manu sneezes. And from that sneeze, from his nostril, a son is born.

`[ON SCREEN: the word इक्ष्वाकु / Ikshvāku in large type]`

That son is Ikshvaku.

Translators often connect the name to *kṣava*, the Sanskrit word for a sneeze. Check that against
your own translation before you say it as fact: the Bhagavata describes the birth itself plainly,
but the word origin comes from later commentary.

## 2:15 – 3:30 · Beat 2: a hundred sons and a map

`[ON SCREEN: a map of northern India, labelled ĀRYĀVARTA. Fifty small flags fade in: twenty-five east, twenty-five west.]`

Ikshvaku becomes king and has a hundred sons. The Bhagavata names the three eldest: Vikukshi, Nimi
and Dandaka.

Then it does something unusual. It says where the sons ruled: twenty-five in the eastern part of
Aryavarta, twenty-five in the western part, three in the middle, and the rest elsewhere.

`[ON SCREEN: three flags in the centre pulse]`

Remember the names Nimi and Dandaka. Nimi's line goes on to produce the kings of Videha, and that
is the family Sita is born into. Dandaka's name lives on in a forest Rama will walk through in
exile: the Dandakaranya. That link is traditional; the Bhagavata does not make it here, so present
it as tradition.

`[ON SCREEN: label changes to TRADITION · NOT SB 9.6 while that line plays]`

But this episode is about the eldest son, Vikukshi, the heir to the throne.

## 3:30 – 5:30 · Beat 3: the rabbit

`[ON SCREEN: label back to SB 9.6. Interior of the palace. A ritual fire is laid but not lit.]`

The king is preparing a rite for the ancestors, an *aṣṭakā śrāddha*: the offering a family makes to
its dead on particular days of the year. In this period, the Bhagavata says plainly, the offering
included meat from the hunt, and the king sends his eldest son to bring it.

`[ON SCREEN: Ikshvaku pointing toward the forest. Vikukshi bows and leaves with a bow and quiver.]`

The instruction is simple: go to the forest and bring back fresh, pure meat for the rite.

Vikukshi goes. He hunts all day. He collects what he needs.

`[ON SCREEN: dusk. The prince sits against a tree, exhausted.]`

And he is exhausted, and starving, and he forgets.

`[ON SCREEN: slow-motion: he takes one rabbit from the pile and eats it.]`

He eats one rabbit, from the meat set aside for the ancestors.

He brings the rest home and hands it over, saying nothing.

`[ON SCREEN: Vasishtha, the family priest, holding his hand over the meat. He stops.]`

Then Vasishtha, the family priest, is asked to sanctify the offering, and he refuses. He tells the
king this meat cannot be offered, because it has already been eaten from. Something offered to the
ancestors has to be untouched, and this has not been.

`[ON SCREEN: Ikshvaku's face, in silence. Hold for two full seconds. No music.]`

The king finds out what his son did.

## 5:30 – 6:45 · Beat 4: the sentence

`[ON SCREEN: throne room. The prince kneels. Ikshvaku stands.]`

Here is the line that makes people stop. For breaking the rule of the rite, Ikshvaku exiles his
eldest son, the heir to the throne, from the kingdom.

`[ON SCREEN: text card: ONE RABBIT. THE WHOLE KINGDOM.]`

It sounds absurdly harsh. So ask the question your viewers are already asking: why?

Here is what the text gives us. The offence was not hunger, and it was not the rabbit. It was that a
prince took a share of the ancestors' offering before they had received it, and then said nothing.
In this story the rite comes before the king's own family, and Ikshvaku is the founder, the one who
sets the rule everyone after him will live by. If the first king bends the rule for his own son, the
dynasty starts out knowing the rules bend.

`[ON SCREEN: label: INTERPRETATION]`

That last part is my reading, not a quotation. The Bhagavata states the facts and the sentence. It
does not explain the sentence, so I will not pretend it does.

## 6:45 – 8:00 · Beat 5: the king walks away

`[ON SCREEN: Ikshvaku and Vasishtha seated together under a tree. Soft light.]`

What happens next turns the story around.

After sending his son away, Ikshvaku does not simply go back to ruling. The Bhagavata says he talks
with Vasishtha about the nature of the self and of the world, and by the end he has become a yogi.
He gives up his body and reaches the Supreme.

`[ON SCREEN: the figure under the tree dissolves into light. The empty throne.]`

The first king of Ayodhya ends his life by walking away from Ayodhya.

And that leaves a throne with no king on it.

## 8:00 – 9:15 · Beat 6: the rabbit-eater comes home

`[ON SCREEN: a lone figure walking back up the road to the city gates.]`

So Vikukshi comes home.

The exiled son returns and becomes king. The Bhagavata says he ruled the earth and performed
sacrifices. And he is remembered by a name he never got rid of:

`[ON SCREEN: शशाद / Śaśāda, then split: śaśa = rabbit · ada = one who eats]`

Shashada, the rabbit-eater.

He is not erased from the family line. He is the next link in it, and his name keeps the reminder.

## 9:15 – 9:50 · Payoff: why Rama is Kakutstha

`[ON SCREEN: a great bull, made of light, with a young warrior seated on its hump.]`

Now for the payoff I promised at the start.

Shashada's son is named Puranjaya. In a war between the gods and the asuras, the gods come to him
for help, and the Bhagavata says Indra himself takes the form of a great bull for him. Puranjaya
rides into battle seated on its hump.

`[ON SCREEN: ककुद् / kakud = the hump  →  काकुत्स्थ / Kākutstha = the one seated on the hump]`

The Sanskrit for that hump is *kakud*. He becomes Kakutstha, the one seated on the hump.

`[ON SCREEN: Rama, bow in hand. Text: "Kākutstha", as Rama is addressed in the Ramayana]`

And when the Ramayana calls Rama "Kakutstha", that is the name it means. It goes back to the
grandson of the prince who ate the rabbit.

## 9:50 – 10:10 · Close

`[ON SCREEN: end card: next episode thumbnail, "Nimi: Ikshvaku's Son Who Lives in Every Blink"]`

But before that battle, there is the other son I asked you to remember: Nimi. He and Vasishtha cursed
each other, he lost his body, and the Bhagavata says that every time you blink, that is Nimi. That is
the next episode, and it is linked right here.
